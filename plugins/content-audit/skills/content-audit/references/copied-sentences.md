# The source's own sentences

The citation defects are about a citation failing to support its claim. This one runs the other way. The claim is correct, the citation is right, and the sentence belongs to the source it came from.

A model working closely from a single source page produces this by default, because the most probable next sentence is the source's next sentence. The result is the source's tip sheet with the order changed, and no automated claim check will notice it.

Matching a source word for word is not verification. When a check for accuracy comes back as a perfect textual match, it has stopped measuring accuracy and started measuring copying.

## Where it concentrates

Sourcing tends to predict correctness: the best-cited sections have the fewest wrong claims. They also carry the most borrowed sentences, because they were written closest to their sources. The two findings are the same finding. The sections that pass the claim check most cleanly are the first ones to read for this.

## How to check

1. Get the source's exact wording. A fetch tool that summarizes returns a paraphrase, which hides the overlap you are looking for. Fetch the raw page (curl, or a browser) and extract its text, or ask the fetch tool for verbatim sentences.
2. Compare distinctive runs: trailing lists, subordinate clauses, and any phrasing more specific than the fact requires. "Clear of bedding, curtains and stacked mail" is a choice someone made. "Three feet" is the fact.
3. Compare the examples and illustrations, along with the sentences.

## The fix is rewriting

Facts, figures, thresholds and distances belong to nobody. They stay intact, along with the attribution. What changes is the expression.

The source's examples are its writing too. Rewording every sentence while keeping the same set of illustrations leaves the borrowing in place: choosing which five household objects illustrate a three-foot clearance is the writing. Choose your own, ideally things the page's actual reader has in the actual room. That usually improves the copy as well.

Its metaphors are its writing most of all. A distinctive analogy is the most authored thing in a paragraph. If a search finds the same analogy attached to the same fact elsewhere, write a different image.

Keep the list when the list is the fact: a statute's prohibited items, a standard's title, an agency's definition of a category, a symptom list. The test is whether a different writer with the same fact could reasonably have chosen different items. If so, choose your own.

## Do not over-correct

Attributed guidance ("the manufacturer says to plug it directly into a wall outlet") is how guidance is supposed to be cited. A measurement or a symptom list has one sensible wording. Rewrite what tracks the source's sentences, and leave alone what merely mentions the source.

Deleting the passage to make the problem go away is the wrong fix. The reader loses a real fact.
